Method

Levelized cost of electricity

With yearly intervals \(t = 1 \dots n\) [1],

\[ \mathrm{LCOE} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n} (I_t + M_t + F_t)\,(1+r)^{-t}}{\displaystyle\sum_{t=\alpha}^{n} E_t\,(1+r)^{-t}} \]

where \(I_t\), \(M_t\) and \(F_t\) ($) are the investment, maintenance and fuel expenditures of year \(t\), \(E_t\) (MWh) the net electricity, \(r\) (per year) the discount rate, \(n\) (years) the lifetime and \(\alpha\) the first year of production. The LCOE is in $/MWh (shown in ¢/kWh: 1 $/MWh = 0.1 ¢/kWh). Defaults [2], in $:

\[ I = N\,h\,c_\mathrm{drill} + c_\mathrm{plant}\,P_\mathrm{gross,max}, \qquad M_t = m\,c_\mathrm{plant}\,P_\mathrm{gross,max}, \qquad F_t = 0 \]
  • \(h = 3000\) m, \(c_\mathrm{drill} = \$1{,}000/\mathrm{m}\) (optimistic: Lukawski et al. give ≈ $2,600/m for a 3 km well [10] [11]), \(c_\mathrm{plant} = \$3{,}000/\mathrm{kW_e}\); \(I\) is spread over \(t < \alpha\).
  • \(N\): number of wells; \(P_\mathrm{gross,max}\) (kWe): the largest monthly gross power, the plant capacity (\(E/8760\) h with a fixed \(E_t\)).
  • \(m = 1.5\,\%\) per year of the plant cost, no wellfield maintenance [16]; \(r = 7\,\%\); \(\alpha = 2\).
  • \(n = \alpha - 1 + 20\) with the model, \(\alpha - 1 + 30\) with a fixed \(E_t\) [9].

From well temperatures to \(E_t\)

For an array of \(N\) wells, the surrogate of GeothermalWells.jl [17] predicts the outlet temperature \(T_i(\tau)\) (°C) of each well \(i\), month by month over 20 years, at a common flow rate \(\dot m\) (kg/s) and inlet temperature \(T_\mathrm{in}\) (°C). For now these temperatures come from a library of 100 arrays of 1–25 wells precomputed with the surrogate; running it directly in the calculator is not yet available. From them:

  1. Heat of each well, \(Q_i\) in W, with \(c_p = 4187.5\) J/(kg·K): \[ Q_i = \dot m\, c_p \left(T_i - T_\mathrm{in}\right) \]
  2. Gross power of the plant, \(P_\mathrm{gross}\) in W, fed with the mixed water at \(T_\mathrm{mix} = \tfrac{1}{N}\sum_i T_i\) (°C); \(\eta\) is dimensionless: \[ P_\mathrm{gross} = \eta(T_\mathrm{mix}) \sum_{i=1}^{N} Q_i, \qquad \eta(T) = \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{0.0935\,T - 2.3266}{100}\right) \] the fit of binary plants at 103–166 °C [4] (\(T\) in °C), extrapolated below 103 °C.
  3. Pumping, \(P_\mathrm{pump}\) in W, with \(\Delta p_i\) (Pa) the friction loss of the inner pipe and the annulus (Darcy–Weisbach, Haaland friction factor [13]), \(\rho\) (kg/m³) the water density [14] and \(\eta_\mathrm{pump} = 0.75\) the pump efficiency: \[ P_\mathrm{pump} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\dot m\, \Delta p_i}{\rho\, \eta_\mathrm{pump}} \] Thermosiphon and surface losses are neglected, so it is an estimate, not a bound.
  4. Net electricity of year \(t\), \(E_t\) in MWh, summed over the 12 months of production year \(t - \alpha + 1\), with \(\Delta\tau = 730\) h (W·h divided by 106): \[ E_t = 10^{-6} \sum_{\tau} \left(P_\mathrm{gross} - P_\mathrm{pump}\right) \Delta\tau, \qquad E_t = 0 \ \text{for}\ t < \alpha \] If the pumps use more than the plant makes, the LCOE is undefined.

Capacity factor (an output, dimensionless; no downtime assumed), with \(E^\mathrm{gross}_t\) (MWh) the gross electricity of year \(t\) and \(P_\mathrm{gross,max}\) in kWe (1 MWh = 103 kWh):

\[ \mathrm{CF} = \frac{1}{n-\alpha+1} \sum_{t=\alpha}^{n} \frac{10^{3}\, E^\mathrm{gross}_t}{8760\ \mathrm{h} \cdot P_\mathrm{gross,max}} \]

Perfect-plant limit. The most electricity any plant could make from the water, its thermodynamic limit [5], \(P_\mathrm{max}\) in W, with a heat sink at \(T_0 = 15\) °C and \(T_c = \max(T_\mathrm{in}, T_0)\); all temperatures in kelvin:

\[ P_\mathrm{max} = N \dot m\, c_p \left[(T_\mathrm{mix} - T_c) - T_0 \ln\frac{T_\mathrm{mix}}{T_c}\right] \ \text{if}\ T_\mathrm{mix} > T_c, \quad 0 \ \text{otherwise} \]

It is used twice: the line under the LCOE gives the LCOE with \(P_\mathrm{gross} = P_\mathrm{max}\) and the same costs and pumping, a what-if on the conversion alone; and a typed \(\eta\) (or the fit) is flagged if \(P_\mathrm{gross}\) exceeds \(P_\mathrm{max}\) in any month.

What to expect

Conventional geothermal costs 6.4–10.6 ¢/kWh [7]; the best closed-loop designs of White et al. reach about 9 ¢/kWh only with 5 km wells, 20 km laterals and 70 °C/km [2]. The library's 3 km wells return water at 11–48 °C, where \(\eta\) is about 2 % (the coolest binary plants in operation reach about 1 % [6]): only cases 72 (about 1,650 ¢/kWh) and 24 make more electricity than their pumps use. These wells suit heat better than electricity [3].
